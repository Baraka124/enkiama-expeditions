Journeys  ·  A few we have already prepared

The route matters.
What happens between places matters more.

These are not sample packages. They are a few real examples of how differently Tanzania can unfold depending on who is travelling, how much time they have, what they are curious about and how they want the days to feel.

A young company, growing personally

One journey has often led to the next.

Enkiama is still young. Much of our early work has come through recommendation: somebody who travelled with us speaking to a friend, a colleague introducing a small group, or a previous conversation becoming a new journey. That is why these examples are useful. They show what we actually do once a trip stops being an idea and becomes a sequence of real days.

01  ·  Hakuna Matata group

A first journey that did not begin with a game drive.

They wanted wildlife, but not a journey that reduced Tanzania to wildlife.

Three travellers arrived through Kilimanjaro and spent the first part of the journey close to the mountain rather than rushing immediately into safari. There were waterfalls, local visits, time around the foothills and days that made room for culture before the route opened into Arusha, Tarangire, Serengeti and Lake Manyara.

Wildlife became one chapter within something broader: landscape, people, food, conversation and the simple pleasure of having enough time to notice where they were.

02  ·  A couple from Spain

The intensity of safari, then time to come down from it.

The coast was not an extension of the safari. It was the counterweight to it.

Their journey began around Kilimanjaro with waterfalls, the mountain base and the geothermal hot springs. Then came Arusha, Tarangire, Ngorongoro and two nights in the Serengeti — enough time for the safari to breathe rather than become a sequence of arrivals and departures.

After the mainland, Stone Town brought history and movement. Nungwi brought several quieter days by the sea. That change of tempo was deliberate: after early starts, long roads and the concentration of safari, the journey needed space.

03  ·  Five travellers, roughly 40–70

A slower journey. And probably the one that explains Enkiama best.

The itinerary had to work for five people, not for an abstract idea of what a safari should be.

Around Moshi and Kilimanjaro there were waterfalls, Chagga communities, coffee-making, village life in Kibosho and lodges chosen as places to enjoy, not simply places to sleep.

Safari days started early when there was a reason to start early: cooler hours, better wildlife activity, first light on the road. But the trip was never built around exhaustion. There was time for a pool, a sunset, a long lunch or simply doing less. Then came Tarangire, Ngorongoro and two nights in the Serengeti.

They saw an extraordinary amount of wildlife, but some of the memories that lasted longest came elsewhere: music in the vehicle; jokes that carried from one day to the next; a Maasai visit that ended with a barbecue rather than a staged stop; and Lake Natron — not initially the headline destination — becoming one of the strongest parts of the journey.

That is the part a map never shows: five people arriving as a group and gradually building their own rhythm together.

04  ·  What changes from one journey to another

The destinations can repeat. The journey should not.

Kilimanjaro, Serengeti or Zanzibar are not products on a shelf. Each can play a different role.

Kilimanjaro might mean a summit attempt, a morning at the foothills or several days around Moshi and Chagga country. Serengeti might be the centre of the trip, or two nights inside a wider route. A Maasai encounter can be a short visit or part of a much longer day. Zanzibar can close a safari, begin a honeymoon or be left out entirely.

We work from the people first, then decide what combination of places, guides, stays and pacing makes sense.
